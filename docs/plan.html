Design notes

Runway Alpha

An agent that finds US airports where a renovation would pay off — places running out of room while demand is still growing. The ranking is arithmetic. The language model routes questions and explains results; it never produces a number.

01

The idea in one paragraph

The question is not which airport is busiest. It is where demand is pushing against a physical limit that building something would relieve. An airport that is busy but has room to spare is a poor investment. One that is cramped but losing passengers is worse. The target is cramped, growing, and structurally under-built.

That idea is written down as a score, in code, so it can be argued with. An analyst who disagrees with the weighting can move a slider and watch all 389 airports re-rank instantly.

02

Scoring methodology

Every airport gets a score from 0 to 100. Five parts, added up, one of them subtracted.

Score = 100 × ( 0.30·Delays + 0.25·Room + 0.20·Growth + 0.15·Unserved − 0.10·Risk ) Delays and congestion = avg departure delay ·.35 + taxi time on a slow day ·.30 + busiest-hour flights per runway ·.35 Running out of room = flights ÷ (runways × 200k) ·.5 + passengers per runway ·.5 Passenger growth = growth per year 2023→25 ·.6 + growth 2024→25 ·.4 Demand it cannot serve = passengers per flight ·.4 + cancelled or diverted ·.3 + people nearby per passenger ·.3 Risk = reliance on one airline ·.5 + delay from weather ·.5 ← subtracted
PartWeightWhy it is in the score
Delays and congestion0.30Delay is what a shortage of capacity actually costs, and it is the best-measured thing available. Highest weight for that reason.
Running out of room0.25How much traffic the airport pushes through the runways it has. Doing a lot with too little is exactly what building fixes.
Passenger growth0.20A terminal pays back over decades. A crowded airport that is shrinking is a trap.
Demand it cannot serve0.15Full planes, cancelled flights, and a large local population flying less than it should.
Risk−0.10Leaning on one airline, or losing hours to weather, both mean new concrete may never convert into more flights.

Five rules that keep the number honest

  • Compare like with like. Each figure is scaled against airports in the same FAA size class, so a small airport is ranked against small airports. Scores from different size classes are not comparable, and the app says so every time it shows them together.
  • Ignore the extremes. Before scaling, values are capped at the 5th and 95th percentile of that size class, using an actual observed value rather than an interpolated one. Without this, one freak airport flattens the scale for everyone else. A test pins it: adding an airport with a 100,000-minute delay must not move a normal airport's congestion score by more than 0.15.
  • Refuse to guess. An airport missing more than one of the five parts gets no score and a stated reason, not an estimate. A missing input inside a part is dropped and the rest re-weighted, which lowers confidence instead of quietly scoring zero.
  • Distrust thin samples. Delay figures are discarded below 500 flights a year. A 90-minute average over 40 flights is a real number and statistically meaningless.
  • Publish the uncertainty. Every score carries a ± derived from how complete its inputs are and how large its sample is. 71 ± 9 and 71 ± 2 are different recommendations.
03

Where AI is used, and where it is not

The model sits at the edges. It reads the question on the way in and writes the answer on the way out. It never touches arithmetic and never sees raw data.

The model does

  • Turn "New England" into a six-state filter, and "LA and Santa Ana" into two airports in the order asked.
  • Choose which of the seven tools answers the question, and with what arguments.
  • Write the prose, and carry the subject across follow-up questions.

The model does not

  • Calculate any score, weight, ranking or percentage.
  • Decide which airport wins a comparison when the measures disagree.
  • See a single row of source data. It only ever sees tool output.

Three mechanisms that enforce this, rather than trusting it

  • The tools are the only route to a number. Each returns its figures together with where they came from and what they do not cover. The same functions are exposed as plain URLs, so any answer can be checked with no model involved at all: /api/rank?region=new_england.
  • Every answer is checked afterwards. Each figure in the reply is pulled out and looked for in the tool output. Anything unaccounted for is flagged on screen next to the answer, not buried in a log.
  • An answer with no tool call is thrown away. If the model produces figures without consulting the data, the server discards the reply and serves the rules-based answer instead, saying that it did.

There is also a button marked Run without the model. It answers the same question using rules over the same tools, with no language model anywhere. It is the offline fallback, and it is the control case: if the two disagree, the model is wrong.

04

How it is built

Public data is pulled once, offline, and boiled down to one small file. Nothing at runtime touches a database.

SourceWhat it givesCatch
BTS On-Time PerformanceEvery US domestic flight in 2024 — delays, taxi time, cancellations, distance, airlineMajor airlines only; no international, no cargo
FAA passenger countsPassengers per airport 2023–25, and the official size class2025 figures are preliminary
OurAirportsRunways, coordinates, airport codesCommunity-maintained, occasionally stale
US CensusPopulation near each airportA 100 km circle, not a real drive time
FAA NAS StatusGround stops happening right nowA snapshot, no history

DuckDB is used only offline, to crunch 7.08 million flight records down to 389 rows in 35 seconds. What ships is a single 480 KB JSON file. It is small enough to hold in memory on the server and send to the browser, which is what lets the weight sliders re-rank every airport with no round trip and no model.

05

Key tradeoffs

Nobody publishes terminal floor space. There is no free national dataset for it. So "terminal expansion" is scored as pressure on flight capacity — inferred, never measured. This is the biggest weakness in the whole thing, and the agent says so whenever a question is about terminals. Real diligence would buy this data.
Historical data, not live. A full year of finished flights beats a fresher partial one for an investment screen, and it makes every answer reproducible. The cost is that the figures are a quarter old, which the agent states.
The weights are an opinion, not a fact. 30/25/20/15/−10 says delay is the strongest evidence of a real constraint. Someone could reasonably weight growth higher. The sliders make that a five-second experiment rather than a code change.
Comparing like with like hides absolute size. A small airport scoring 70 and a large one scoring 70 do not represent the same amount of money. The raw figures are always shown next to the score so an analyst can judge that themselves.
No costs, no land, no politics. The score measures upside only. It produces a shortlist for a human to underwrite; it does not price a deal or say whether anything can actually be built.
Small airports have thinner data. Only the larger airlines report delay data, so regional airports are measured on less. That shows up as a wider uncertainty band, rather than being smoothed over.
06

What changed once the data arrived

Three sources in the original plan did not survive contact. Recorded here rather than quietly edited out, because the substitutions affect how much the numbers can be trusted.

PlannedWhat happenedDone instead
BTS T-100
for seats and load factor		 Every download endpoint now returns 404. No free source carries seat counts per route. Load is approximated by passengers per flight. That mixes up how full planes are with how big they are, so it is labelled an approximation everywhere it appears.
Census API
for local population		 It now requires a registered key. Census bulk files, which do not. The whole data pipeline still runs with no credentials of any kind.
FAA forecasts
in the growth score		 Reachable, but it is the FAA forecasting its own future — a planning document, not a market view. Dropped. Growth now rests on two measured passenger changes. Less forward-looking, entirely observed.

One bug worth naming: the FAA files airports under its own identifier, not the code on your boarding pass. Mesa Gateway is AZA to travellers and IWA to the FAA. Two airports silently lost all their passenger data until the join matched on either.

Every figure the agent reports carries its source and its uncertainty. Where the data cannot answer, it says so instead of estimating.
